The Devastating Style of Hsing-I

AuthorSifu James W. McNeil, Gate Keeper of the Little Nine Heaven Wu Tao System
SourceL9H Internal Kung-Fu newsletter, 2015
CoversOrigins · Eight Fundamentals · Seven Stars · Five Elements · Horse form

This article is adapted from Sifu James W. McNeil's four-part newsletter series on Hsing-I Chuan, lightly edited for the web. Sifu McNeil is the teacher of Sifu Ajay Kumra, who teaches Hsing-I privately in Santee, California.

Origins

General Fueh Fei (Yue Fei) is given credit for developing the devastating style of Hsing-I during the period of the Northern Sung dynasty (A.D. 960–1276). General Fueh was a faithful and loyal fighter in the wars against the foreign tribes. He learned Hsing-I and passed the style on to his troops, giving rise to the saying, "it is easier to fight a mountain than to fight the army of General Fueh Fei." He was later jailed and put to death for political reasons.

After Fueh Fei, the art was passed on by Chi Lung Feng, who was born at the end of the Ming dynasty. He was honest and generous, and his fighting skills were good, but he was not satisfied, because he believed there was something better. He traveled extensively between 1638 and 1661 seeking a teacher with the best fighting skills. His dream came true when he met an enigmatic Taoist near the foot of the Chung Nan Mountains in Shensi province, who taught him and gave him an instructional book called Hsing-I, authored and passed on by General Fueh Fei. Chi studied and practiced day and night until he had mastered all the movements, and after that no one was able to match his fighting skills.

What makes this style so devastating? The Eight Fundamentals of Hsing-I, the theories of touch-go-kiss and true-not-true, and the seven stars, along with the power of the five elements. They follow the laws of nature, and that is what makes them devastating: there is no power greater than the elements, for they constitute all power.

The Eight Fundamentals and San Ti Posture

The Eight Fundamentals, sometimes called the "Eight Words" of Hsing-I, give the practitioner a picture of the correct posture and alignment that must be mastered in every technique. Each word contains three key points, making twenty-four points to observe throughout practice. They can be studied one at a time, but their true strength appears only when they are combined as one.

Externally, correct alignment makes each posture strong from the foundations upward and lets the practitioner issue and dissolve power efficiently when fighting. Internally, it lets the meridians, vessels, bones and organs sit in their correct positions, so that chi and blood flow smoothly, which is the key to good health. The Eight Fundamentals are first developed in standing practice in the San Ti posture, and later in every movement of Hsing-I. The sections below cover four of the eight.

The Three Press-Ups. The head is the headquarters of the body: when it lifts, chi can travel up the back to the crown. Keep the neck slightly stretched but not tense, keep the head still when moving, and do not tense the face or jaw. The tongue, pressed lightly against the palate, helps chi sink to the tan tien; it acts as a switch connecting the Du and Ren meridians to complete the circuit known as the "Little Nine Heaven" circulation. In a fight it also keeps the tongue from being bitten. The palm, pressed outward, extends chi through the body and strengthens the fingers and arms. The index finger of the forward hand stretches up to lead the chi. No part of the hand should be too relaxed or too tense; this feeling is called "spirited" or "alive."

The Three Suppresses. The shoulders sink slightly inward and downward so the chest feels empty and strength flows to the elbows, like tensioning the two ends of a bow. This relaxes the chest for breathing through the tan tien and brings the elbows in to protect the ribs and heart. The backs of the hands and feet are suppressed, which makes them stronger: the hands are poised to grab, and the feet grip the ground to root the body. The teeth and chin suppress downward so the jaw is firm, protecting the head and neck in a fight.

The Three Rounds. The back is slightly rounded with the suppressed shoulders, giving the posture of "bear's shoulders" and letting power pass easily through the waist into the arms. The chest is rounded in a concave, empty way to join with the back and shoulders, giving full strength when needed and smooth, relaxed breathing. The hands are rounded at the "tiger's mouth" and through all the fingers, ready to seize the opponent, and the rounded joints let chi and blood flow freely.

The Three Sensitives. The eyes are windows that can give away one's feelings; facing an opponent, they must be confident, fierce and alert so the mind can read the situation quickly. The heart, which in Chinese medicine governs the mind, must be balanced with the wisdom of the brain. The hands, trained to "feel" and "listen," eventually become like eyes. Too much tension in the hands wastes strength and costs a beat of time, because they must relax before they can move.

Whilst this skill can be discussed in theory, the novice practitioner needs the attention of a knowledgeable teacher to truly master this feeling.

The Body of Hsing-I

We practice the martial arts for self-defense, good health and longevity. In Hsing-I we use light and powerfully mobile chicken legs and the indomitable strength of the dragon body, combined with the engulfing, crushing force of bear shoulders and the slashing grip of the eagle's claws. The body moves like a monkey: never leaning forward or back, always centered for leaping, balanced movement. Seen from the front, the body should look as if it faces the side; seen from the side, it should seem to face the front. This subtle compromise in positioning can only be learned from a competent teacher.

The front leg is light and the back leg solid. The legs are curved just so: too straight is easy to break, too bent is unstable and immobile. The force of the hand is in the wrist and its power in the fingers. The shoulders are dropped and relaxed; raised shoulders become stiff and must relax again before a full attack, costing precious seconds in a real fight.

In regular practice, visualize facing a top opponent; this trains the heart for fierceness and courage. At the same time, cultivate emptiness through meditation, so that in a real fight the response is instinctive, with relaxed power that echoes and tracks the opponent's movement. Flexibility matters too: the tighter the tendon, the shorter the life span, which is why external and internal exercises should always be combined. The first and most important point is to learn to use the breath. The road is not smooth or easy, and only perseverance and experienced instruction lead to the destination.

Soft Power, Seven Stars and Touch-Go-Kiss

Hsing-I is a soft style: receiving and manipulating the opponent is just as important as striking. The opponent's force is used against him, drawing him into a vortex as you step close into a position of dominance. The strike is relaxed and drives completely through and beyond the target. Because of the danger to human life in this system, defeating someone is not the most important aspect; it is balancing a respect for all life with the desire to preserve your own.

All power is generated from the tan tien, working with an unbroken root, so that power travels from the foot through every joint and out to the fingertips. A seven stars strike follows the chain the other way, beginning with the head and following through every joint to the foot. The head, shoulder, elbow, hand, hip, knee and foot are the seven stars, all weapons that can strike with great power.

Hsing-I is a defensive style in which the opponent is often allowed the first move and drawn into a counterattack, combined with street-fighting theories that allow attacking first against multiple opponents. From this come the principles of touch-go-kiss and true-not-true, the ability to change a technique mid-move. As the opponent attacks, you "touch" the strike aside (a miss is a miss, so why waste energy blocking too forcefully?), at the same moment "go" deep into his guard with the whole body moving as one, and "kiss," engaging him with any of the seven stars. Moving in close allows deceptive short-range power that most styles cannot duplicate. With the right timing, the opponent's force combines with yours and can more than double your striking power.

  • Move as if a flame runs over your body and you are moving to put it out.
  • The front hand aligns with the nose; the rear hand centers on the chest. The hands stay inside the body's perimeter, along the centerline.
  • One hand goes out as the other returns, with matching force and speed.
  • To strike is to go all out, hands and legs moving together, with few feints. Elbows stay in and low to protect the body.
  • Steps are firm, with strength rooted from the foot. Never use leg fakes; kick only when a full-power strike to an opening is available.

Every movement contains yin and yang, true and not true: creative and destructive, long and short. The forms are healthful for your own body and destructive to the opponent, and they contain the power to heal internal maladies.

The Five Elements

Hsing-I consists of the five elements: metal, water, wood, fire and earth. Each has its own forms and its own quality of movement and chi, and the student must master these qualities before true Hsing-I power can be felt.

  • Metal splits: a strike that rises and falls like an ax, swift, direct and unstoppable.
  • Water drills: chi like a column of water under great force, best seen in a riptide where currents flow against yet feed each other. You receive the opponent's force and complete the circle like a whirlpool.
  • Wood crushes: chi like an arrow released from a bow. The arm is like a tree limb, solid yet flexible, and the fist flies like an arrow and penetrates deep.
  • Fire pounds: chi like the firing of a cannon. Fire is warmth and passion, but run wild it engulfs everything. Each hit must land like a cannonball, with a clear mind and careful aim.
  • Earth, the mother of all elements, crosses: chi rolling and round like a marble. Perfect alignment makes you centered and stable as a mountain, yet light enough to turn with ease.

Each element also corresponds to organs of the body, the solid yin organs and the hollow yang organs, which is why the forms work as medicine as well as fighting methods.

Why the Old Forms Are Preserved

Every movement and position has a meaning and has been preserved through time for a purpose; whether the student understands it depends on the quality of instruction and the length of study. Forms work the chi through specific patterns in the body, and herein lies the magic. A skilled practitioner can make changes that suit himself, but he is in no position to improve on centuries of practice when teaching others, and trying to do so often means the magic is lost. Most beginners' mistakes concern balance: stay rooted, keep your back straight, and always be centered, solid and stable.

The Twelve Animals and the Horse Form

In the twelve animal forms, the spirit (hsing) of the animal matters more than whether the form looks like the animal. Lineages differ in movements and names, and none is simply better than another; what unites them as one family is the spirit expressed. The twelve animals in Sifu McNeil's Shansi lineage are Dragon, Tiger, Monkey, Horse, Tow, Cock, Phoenix, Sparrow/Hawk, Swallow, Snake, Eagle/Bear and Fighting Chicken.

The Horse, or Ma, is fast and powerful, with great stamina and a strong heart. In battle a horse rises on its hind legs and strikes repeatedly with its front hooves. In the form, the weight stays heavily over the back foot and that loaded power drives straight forward from the ground, up through the body and out through the arms. Like a horse bolting through a gap in a fence, the practitioner watches for a gap in the opponent's defense and drives straight through it. The Horse belongs to the element Fire and shares its twisting forearm action. It aligns with the heart: practiced smoothly, it strengthens the heart's chi, calms the emotions and breath, and lets the practitioner think clearly and act honorably without rash anger.

Competent instruction is required, and attention to detail is necessary, when practicing any Hsing-I form in order to obtain the health benefits.
