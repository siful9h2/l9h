The Devastating Style of Hsing-I, Part 4: The Five Elements and the Horse Form

Adapted for the web from Sifu James W. McNeil's newsletter, lightly edited. Sifu McNeil is the Gate Keeper of the Little Nine Heaven Wu Tao System and the teacher of Sifu Ajay Kumra, who teaches privately in Santee, California.

The Five Elements

Hsing-I consists of the five elements: metal, water, wood, fire and earth. Each has its own forms and its own quality of movement and chi, and the student must master these qualities before true Hsing-I power can be felt.

  • Metal splits: a strike that rises and falls like an ax, swift, direct and unstoppable.
  • Water drills: chi like a column of water under great force, best seen in a riptide where currents flow against yet feed each other. You receive the opponent's force and complete the circle like a whirlpool.
  • Wood crushes: chi like an arrow released from a bow. The arm is like a tree limb, solid yet flexible, and the fist flies like an arrow and penetrates deep.
  • Fire pounds: chi like the firing of a cannon. Fire is warmth and passion, but run wild it engulfs everything. Each hit must land like a cannonball, with a clear mind and careful aim.
  • Earth, the mother of all elements, crosses: chi rolling and round like a marble. Perfect alignment makes you centered and stable as a mountain, yet light enough to turn with ease.

Each element also corresponds to organs of the body, the solid yin organs and the hollow yang organs, which is why the forms work as medicine as well as fighting methods.

Why the Old Forms Are Preserved

Every movement and position has a meaning and has been preserved through time for a purpose; whether the student understands it depends on the quality of instruction and the length of study. Forms work the chi through specific patterns in the body, and herein lies the magic. A skilled practitioner can make changes that suit himself, but he is in no position to improve on centuries of practice when teaching others, and trying to do so often means the magic is lost. Most beginners' mistakes concern balance: stay rooted, keep your back straight, and always be centered, solid and stable.

The Twelve Animals and the Horse Form

In the twelve animal forms, the spirit (hsing) of the animal matters more than whether the form looks like the animal. Lineages differ in movements and names, and none is simply better than another; what unites them as one family is the spirit expressed. The twelve animals in Sifu McNeil's Shansi lineage are Dragon, Tiger, Monkey, Horse, Tow, Cock, Phoenix, Sparrow/Hawk, Swallow, Snake, Eagle/Bear and Fighting Chicken.

The Horse, or Ma, is fast and powerful, with great stamina and a strong heart. In battle a horse rises on its hind legs and strikes repeatedly with its front hooves. In the form, the weight stays heavily over the back foot and that loaded power drives straight forward from the ground, up through the body and out through the arms. Like a horse bolting through a gap in a fence, the practitioner watches for a gap in the opponent's defense and drives straight through it. The Horse belongs to the element Fire and shares its twisting forearm action. It aligns with the heart: practiced smoothly, it strengthens the heart's chi, calms the emotions and breath, and lets the practitioner think clearly and act honorably without rash anger.

Competent instruction is required, and attention to detail is necessary, when practicing any Hsing-I form in order to obtain the health benefits.
