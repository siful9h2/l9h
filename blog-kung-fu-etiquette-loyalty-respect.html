Kung Fu Etiquette: Loyalty, Respect and the Bow

AuthorSifu James W. McNeil
First publishedL9H newsletters, February 2009 (Volume 2, Issue 2) and January 2021

Adapted for the web from Sifu James W. McNeil's newsletter, lightly edited. Sifu McNeil is the Gate Keeper of the Little Nine Heaven Wu Tao System and the teacher of Sifu Ajay Kumra, who teaches privately in Santee, California.

Loyalty, etiquette, respect and honesty are the simple things that many mothers, fathers, men, women, friends, students and, most of all, teachers have lacked throughout the years. These days, it seems the lack of respect and etiquette is getting worse, not better. Ninety-nine percent of my students have shown great loyalty, respect, honesty and proper etiquette towards me and towards our traditions. There are only a few who make it look bad for the others.

Loyalty

True loyalty and respect are earned. They cannot be bought, sold or given away. To respect someone is to trust them fully, without worry, without lies, hatred or false pretense. Loyalty and respect are a special kind of bond between people and traditions, and between students and teacher.

Etiquette

The one thing that many of us have failed at achieving, one time or another, is proper etiquette and respect for our mothers, fathers, friends, teachers, martial arts brothers and sisters, and our students. Throughout Chinese and Taoist culture, etiquette and respect have been especially important and necessary. Some of us do not have a martial arts school or a place to teach others, but we do have martial arts brothers and sisters with whom we have trained in the past and meet occasionally, either on the street or at a seminar. The same etiquette and respect should continue no matter where we meet. Always learn the proper etiquette when visiting another country.

Being humble and using proper etiquette is a big part of kung fu training. It is part of your moral character training, in which your Sifu will test to see if you are worthy of learning the more advanced levels of kung fu. If he thinks you have a poor attitude, you may never learn the deeper aspects of kung fu.

Not too long ago, I was teaching a seminar and had a senior student teach less than one hour of Splashing Hands to the junior students. A junior student at this seminar was very upset that his senior brother led the class, as he felt he should have done it. A teacher should never have to explain to his students what he decides or why. The teacher should never be questioned in public. If he is questioned, it should be done in private. The rules of etiquette and respect help build character, offer structure to the beginner, and provide depth and meaning for the more advanced student.

The Bow and the Salute

The first place to show etiquette is with the bow or salute when entering or leaving the kung fu school. One of the main reasons to bow or salute when you enter a school is to give honor and respect to the past masters who have gone before you, for the hardships they endured to gain their skill and knowledge. Saluting or bowing to your senior brothers and sisters also shows humility.

Always bow or salute to your Sifu, and give him or her the utmost respect for the hardships they have gone through to achieve their position. Also salute the senior students and any classmates you may be working with in push hands or fighting drills. This shows them that you bear no hard feelings during training, especially during fighting applications. Salute at the beginning and end of the exercise.

The salute or bow is a sign of respect, and there are many types of salutes. Many Shaolin schools use the right fist with the left palm covering it, arms extended in front of you. This means respect, peace and honor to the person you are saluting, whether he or she is the Sifu, a student, or the kung fu school itself. It shows respect all the way from the five oceans to the five seas. Stand straight up as you salute when entering the school or greeting a teacher. Always bend forward a little when saluting your senior brothers or sisters, and bend more for your Sifu.

Respect

Be gracious and humble and always show respect to others. This is the first part of the creed, and for good reason. Respect is a big part of your martial arts training, and it is not only for your teacher, but for your family, students and friends. Always say "Yes, Sifu" or "No, Sifu," "Please" and "Thank you." Never make a demand that your teacher must teach you or that he owes you. When eating, always make sure that your Sifu eats first; never start before or without him. These signs of respect build and overflow into regular life. Overall, respect is essential for all martial arts students and their goal to master themselves before they can teach others.

When you are invited into a friend's, student's or teacher's home or hotel, or even if he or she is staying in your home, you should never look at or go through their written materials, books, computer, or anything else for that matter. Never ask what they are doing or writing. If they wanted you to know, they would tell you.

Always remember your teacher, your family and your friends on holidays with a special card or gift to let them know you appreciate all that they have done for you. Always be polite and respect all foreigners. If your teacher leaves while teaching you, continue practicing and never stop until he or she returns and tells you to stop or change. Never question your teacher as to why he is late to your class. Never pester or demand that your teacher teach you more. He will teach you when you are ready.

There are a few things that should never be said to your teacher:

  • "I do not have enough room to practice."
  • "I didn't have time to practice this week because I was busy."
  • "My back, legs or body hurts, and I am tired today. Can I take it easy?"

Think what would have happened if your teacher had given these excuses to his masters.

Honesty

Honesty is the best policy. In these times, money is tight and hard to come by no matter who you are. As a teacher, you must respect your students and treat them equally no matter what their status or wealth, and most of all, be honest with them. Charging school brothers to train and practice together should never be tolerated, as you are a martial arts family, and who charges family? Making people believe you can do things that only your teacher can do should never be done, as it is lying.

The School Altar

Many schools have a designated area to honor the school's ancestors. Frequently there will be a small statue of General Kwan or Huang-Ti, or paintings or photographs of past masters. Although not specifically religious in nature, it is an area that must be regarded with a great deal of respect. If you wish to light incense at the altar to show your respect, you may do so, but avoid touching, bumping or disturbing the altar. Altars come in many forms and styles, from a small stand on a table to a large altar that covers an entire wall.

In Chinese culture, showing respect for your ancestors is a major part of family life. The same can be said for the kung fu family. Showing respect to the men and women who have gone ahead of you is a major part of respect for the kung fu student.

To sum this all up: be honest, be sincere, think of others, and treat them as you would like to be treated. This is what I teach, it is the way of the Tao, and it is the way most of my students believe in. I want to thank each one of my students who have been loyal to our system and to the masters who preserved it and passed down their knowledge to me, so I can pass that knowledge on to my students.
