Hsing-I Seven Stars & Touch-Go-Kiss

AuthorSifu James W. McNeil
First publishedL9H Internal Kung-Fu newsletter, November 2015 (Volume 8, Issue 11)
SeriesThe Devastating Style of Hsing-I · Part 3

Adapted for the web from Sifu James W. McNeil's newsletter, lightly edited. Sifu McNeil is the Gate Keeper of the Little Nine Heaven Wu Tao System and the teacher of Sifu Ajay Kumra, who teaches privately in Santee, California.

We practice the martial arts for self-defense, good health and longevity, but this practice is not easily explained in so few words. No matter how it is changed, a style cannot pull away from its origins. In Hsing-I, we use light and powerfully mobile chicken legs and the indomitable strength of the dragon body. This is combined with the engulfing, crushing force of bear shoulders and the slashing grip of the eagle's claws. The body's movements should be like a monkey; it should not lean forward or back, but always be centered in itself for leaping, balanced movement. When viewing the body from the front, it should look as if it was facing the side, but from the side, it should seem to face the front. This is an extremely subtle compromise in body positioning and can only be learned from a competent teacher. The front leg should be light, the back leg solid. The legs should be curved just so; too straight is easy to break, and too bent is unstable and immobile. The force of the hand should be in the wrist, its power in the fingers. The shoulders should be dropped and relaxed; too high and they become stiff, forcing you to relax them again to move quickly into a full devastating attack, costing precious seconds in the reality of a fight.

During regular practice, one should visualize facing a top opponent. This trains the heart for fierceness and courage. However, the practitioner must cultivate emptiness through meditation so that in a real fight the response will be instinctive, with relaxed power, echoing and tracking the opponent's movement. When attacking, step forward and always be oriented to the center gate; avoid over-reaching by maintaining the body's perimeter with the hands and body moving and reaching together. The hands should be alive and moving and the legs light and mobile. Use the nose for orienting and targeting, like the cross hairs on a rifle scope, throughout your movements. Attacking is attacking; retreating is retreating, although one can make efficient and effective strikes while withdrawing. Every step and strike should be coordinated to avoid being unbalanced and losing the advantage to the opponent.

The saying "hurting one's fist while punching others and one's feet while kicking others" warns us that even though we may try all types of attack, we may not always strike with effect or subdue our enemy. Strength applied in self-defense must be sufficient to hurt the opponent. There are no absolute methods that guarantee the weak will overcome the strong; therefore, we must have some basic training to guarantee surer, swifter and better methods of self-defense. This also provides the reasoning behind using basic exercises for body conditioning to improve one's life. It is essential to keep the body in top condition to achieve the best results from any technique.

Flexibility is important because the more flexible the body, the more free and fluid the movements. The tighter the tendon, the shorter the life span, which is why you should always combine external and internal exercises. The first and most important point is to learn to utilize the breath. Relaxing the body and mind produces movements that are natural and flowing. All of your actions will become natural and ultimately the will uses a thought to move the body. The road is not a smooth and easy one, and only with perseverance and experienced instruction can one reach the destination.

Hsing-I is a soft style, which means receiving and manipulating the opponent is just as important as striking. The opponent's force is used against him, sucking him into a vortex of whirling destruction. Every vital spot is a target in one furious barrage as you step close into a position of dominance. The circle is completed and maximum power is transferred. The strike is relaxed and drives completely through and beyond the target. Because of the inherent power and danger to human life in this system, defeating someone is not the most important aspect. Rather, it is balancing a respect for all life with respect for and desire to preserve your own.

Hsing-I is a powerful and practical self-defense fighting style. All power is generated from the tan tien, which is the center of the body's balance and energy. This energy works in conjunction with an unbroken root to the ground as the power comes from the foot, through every joint and out to the fingertips. Conversely, a seven stars strike follows a chain of movements, in whole or in part, beginning with the head and following through with every joint to end at the foot. This is the basis of the theory of the seven stars of Hsing-I: the head, shoulder, elbow, hand, hip, knee and foot are all weapons of the human body that can strike with mortal power.

Hsing-I is a defensive style in which the opponent many times is allowed the first move and is drawn into a counterattack. However, this is combined with our street fighting theories, which allow for attacking first in multiple opponent situations. The defensive theory gives birth to the principles of touch-go-kiss and true, not true, or the ability to alter a technique in mid-move in response to the opponent's actions. As the opponent attacks, one must take the first step to deflect or "touch" the strike away (a miss is a miss; why spend energy by blocking too forcefully?) and at the same time "go," or step deeply into the opponent's guard, the whole body moving at one time, close enough to "kiss," or engage him, by using any of the seven stars techniques, singly or in combination. Moving in close to the opponent allows the delivery of deceptive short-range power that most other styles cannot duplicate. This takes speed, delicate sensitivity in order to deflect incoming force and, above all else, practice. The proper timing can more than double your striking power as the opponent's force combines with yours.

With these seven rules come the means by which you will successfully deliver your attack:

  • To strike is to go "all out" and to move the hands and legs together. Feints and fakes are minimal. The fists are as cannons, the body like that of a dragon, turning, twisting, alive and deadly. The expulsion of breath is as the sound of thunder from within as the chi fills the body and begs for release through the legs.
  • Move when you face an attacker as if you have a flame running through and over your body and you are moving in order to put it out.
  • The front hand is aligned with the nose to appear hidden from the front. The rear hand centers on the chest and seems concealed from the side. The hands assume position within the body's perimeter, inside the "box" along the "centerline," and rarely deviate.
  • Moving from your chest, the hands are like a powerful tiger catching a delicate lamb. Elbows are in and lowered to better protect the body and explode out with power.
  • There is a counter-balance between the hands. One hand goes out; the other returns at a matching force and speed. An effective technique is twisting the wrist into the target for internal penetration. It can be turned like a corkscrew or torqued vertically up and down. This must be practiced cautiously. (There is nothing worse than losing a fight by breaking a wrist.)
  • Knee strikes to vital points can be fatal. Hands are up and slightly out to help balance the movements of the body.
  • Steps are firm, with the strength coming from the foot rooted to the ground. Never use leg fakes; this only destroys your commitment and breaks your root unnecessarily. Only kick when a full-powered strike to a vital spot is open.

An advanced technique known as the "shock punch" is a method of punching which delivers a great internal trauma to the opponent. The shock is generated by a driving motion of the hips, rooting and thrusting out at the same moment the punch contacts the opponent.

When chi generated erupts from the tan tien, power is produced to work the forge of the will. The power is held within until it is used and is self-regenerating. Every movement contains the theory of yin and yang, or true not true. Every move contains creative and destructive attributes and combines long and short. The forms are healthful and beneficial to your body and destructive to the opponent, and contain the power to heal internal maladies within your body.
