Splashing Hands

OriginEarly 1700s, northern China
CharacterFast, close, relentlessly forward
KicksLow — knee height and below
Lineage HereThrough Master Haumea Lefiti

Splashing Hands is the least decorative art we teach. There is no performance version of it. Everything in the system exists because it settles a fight quickly, and anything that did not serve that purpose was discarded somewhere in the last three hundred years.

The name describes the hands: strikes arrive in continuous overlapping waves rather than as single committed blows, each one setting up the next, none of them waiting for the last to finish. Against an opponent expecting a punch-and-reset rhythm, the effect is of being caught under something that keeps coming.

How It Fights

  • Forward pressure. Splashing Hands does not wait, circle, or trade at range. It closes and stays closed.
  • Low kicks only. Shins, knees, thighs, groin. Nothing above the waist, because nothing above the waist is reliable when the ground is uneven and the situation is real.
  • Shuffling footwork. Feet stay under the body and near the floor, which keeps the base intact while the hands work.
  • Hand combinations over single strikes. The system assumes the first strike will not end it.
Master Lefiti taught this art to people who needed it. That inheritance shapes how it is still taught — plainly, without mystique, and with attention to what actually happens under pressure.

Forms and Two-Man Sets

The curriculum includes both solo forms and two-man fighting sets. The two-man work is where the art is really learned: timing, distance, and the willingness to keep moving forward are not things a solo form can teach you.

Who It Suits

Students who want self-defence they can use within a reasonable timeframe usually start here. It also pairs unusually well with the internal systems — the footwork and hand mechanics share more with Tzu Men Chuan than either art's reputation would suggest.
